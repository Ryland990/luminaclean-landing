How to Find Large Videos on iPhone

The fastest way to find large videos on iPhone is to check Settings > General > iPhone Storage first, then open Photos > Albums > Media Types > Videos and tap the info button on clips to see file size. A 10-minute 4K/60fps video can take roughly 4 GB, and one hour can reach about 17 GB (storage guide).

That's why a phone can feel full even when the camera roll doesn't look outrageous. A few long clips, a burst of 4K recording, or one ProRes file can crowd out updates, messages, and new photos before the owner realizes what happened. Apple's own storage tools help, but the Photos app still doesn't give a true size-sorted video list, so the trick is knowing where the native shortcuts end and where manual review begins.

Why Your iPhone Storage Suddenly Feels Full

The warning often appears when a new clip will not save or an iOS update cannot start. Video can fill storage quickly, especially when recording settings stay high. 4K at 30 fps uses about 170 MB per minute, while 4K at 60 fps uses about 400 MB per minute (storage guide).

Why the camera roll lies

A camera roll full of familiar thumbnails can still contain a serious storage problem. A 2026 study reported a median library with 2,547 photos and 127 videos. Videos represented only 5% of items but used 42% of photo-library storage, while photos and videos together accounted for 38% of total iPhone storage (study).

Practical rule: if storage feels tight, check video before blaming apps or messages.

File size also depends on how much space the phone has available. A 128 GB iPhone typically offers about 85 GB of usable space after the operating system and other reserved data. A few long recordings can therefore create a storage emergency well before the capacity label appears to be reached.

A useful comparison is a single 10-minute recording. At about 4 GB, a 4K/60fps clip consumes nearly 5% of the roughly 85 GB usable on a 128 GB iPhone. The recording itself may be easy to miss among everyday photos, screenshots, and short clips.

Checking iPhone Storage for Video Pressure

Open Settings > General > iPhone Storage before browsing your camera roll. This screen ranks apps and categories by local storage use, giving you a quick way to decide whether videos deserve attention or whether another category is responsible.

What to read on the storage screen

Look at the size of Photos, not just the total capacity bar. A large Photos category points toward media review. If Photos is lower on the list, check messages, app data, downloads, and other categories before deleting videos.

The screen can also show Apple's cleanup recommendations. Treat them as prompts, not a complete file-finding system. iPhone Storage identifies where space is concentrated, but it does not give you a reliable size-sorted list of videos. That gap is why a library can require manual checking even after you identify Photos as the main source of pressure.

Use a focused check before reviewing everything. If Photos is consuming 15 to 20 GB on a 128 GB device, open Photos, go to Albums > Videos, and inspect the 3 to 5 longest clips first. A 20-minute 4K/60fps recording can exceed 8 GB, so duration provides a useful starting filter, even though recording format still affects the final size.

How to separate local clutter from cloud-managed media

Check whether Optimize iPhone Storage is enabled. Apple explains that this setting keeps full-resolution photos and videos in iCloud while smaller device copies remain on the iPhone when needed (Apple support). With optimization off, the phone may store the original files locally, so deleting or moving large clips can produce a more noticeable change.

iCloud Photos does not make deletion immediate in every case. Removed items can remain in Recently Deleted during Apple's recovery period, so available space may not increase until that folder is cleared. Follow this guide to check whether Recently Deleted photos still use storage.

Storage screens are diagnostic tools, not cleanup tools. They show where the pressure sits, then you choose which files to review.

Use the result to choose your next action. A small Photos category points away from video. A large one justifies a targeted video review, while the absence of native size sorting means you should avoid wasting time opening clips at random.

Using the Photos App and Files to Locate Big Clips

Apple's Photos app makes video browsing easy and size sorting awkward. It can filter by media type and show your Videos collection, but it doesn't offer a built-in sort by file size control in Albums (Apple support). That's the gap many users run into when they search for how to find large videos on iPhone.

The native path in Photos

The direct path is simple. Open a video, tap the Information button at the bottom, and Apple shows the file size in the details view under the device name (Apple support). That makes it possible to verify one clip at a time, which is fine when the library is small or the suspect list is short.

The catch is obvious. Manual inspection is slow, and duration can be misleading. Apple notes that ProRes files can be up to 30 times larger than HEVC files (Apple support). So a short ProRes clip can easily outweigh a longer standard recording.

When Files helps more than Photos

Files is useful when videos have already been exported there, because size-based sorting becomes more practical outside the Photos app. That's a better fit when someone needs to compare many clips quickly, especially after moving a batch out of the camera roll. Photos is still the cleaner place to start, because it preserves the native library and keeps you close to the source.

Format Approximate Size per Minute Best For
4K at 30 fps about 170 MB Higher-quality recording with less pressure than 60 fps
4K at 60 fps about 400 MB Fast motion and smoother playback
ProRes much larger than HEVC, up to 30 times larger High-value recording, editing workflows

The safest triage rule is to stop trusting clip length. Check the info panel on suspicious videos, especially screen recordings, high-frame-rate clips, and anything shot in ProRes. The longest file is not always the biggest one.

Choosing the Right Cleanup Approach for Your Situation

The right method depends on library size, urgency, and how much trust the user wants to place in automation. A few oversized clips call for a different fix than a camera roll that's been growing for years. The fastest approach is often a blend of native diagnostics and a focused cleanup tool.

Pick the method that matches the problem

If the phone just started complaining and the library is manageable, Apple's own tools are enough. Settings > General > iPhone Storage shows whether Photos is the pressure point, and Photos > Videos lets the user confirm individual file sizes one by one. That works when there are only a few obvious candidates.

If the library is large, manual review turns into a slog. Exporting clips into Files can help with size sorting, but it adds steps and still requires moving content out of Photos. For people with years of 4K clips or lots of high-bitrate recordings, that workflow gets old fast.
